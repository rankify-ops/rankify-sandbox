If you've got rooftop solar and a tired electric hot water tank, a heat pump is one of the easiest ways to use more of your own power. Set it to run in the middle of the day and your panels heat the water, rather than exporting cheap solar and buying it back in the evening. Here's how it works, what the official feed-in tariffs say about the maths, and which models in our range make it easy. Official pages were checked on **25 October 2026**.

## The short answer

- **Yes, a heat pump runs happily on solar.** It plugs into your normal household electricity, so surplus solar powers it automatically while the panels are producing.
- **The trick is timing.** Use the built-in timer, app or PV input to run it in the middle of the day. The [Clean Energy Regulator](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-renewable-energy-systems/solar-water-heaters-and-air-source-heat-pumps) recommends exactly this for households with rooftop solar.
- **You don't need a big system.** The heat pumps in our range are rated at roughly **0.55 to 1 kW** while running, and heat a typical family's water with a few kWh a day.
- **Self-consumption beats exporting.** 2026–27 feed-in tariffs are low (for example **6.006 c/kWh** in regional Queensland), so using your solar is usually worth several times more than selling it.

## How a heat pump uses your solar

A heat pump moves heat from the air into the water using a small compressor, so it needs only a fraction of the electricity an electric element would. The CER describes an air source heat pump as being powered by your normal household wiring or your solar panels, and says that if you have rooftop solar it lets you use excess solar generated during the day to run your hot water. Victoria's [Energy Upgrades hot water page](https://www.energy.vic.gov.au/households/victorian-energy-upgrades-for-households/hot-water-systems) makes the same point: an efficient electric hot water system can use excess solar during the day to heat water.

Think of the tank as a battery for heat. You fill it with hot water while the sun's out, and it keeps that water hot for the evening showers and the next morning. iStore, for example, says its 270L stores up to **16.34 kWh** of usable thermal energy ([iStore 270L page](https://heatpumps.istore.net.au/270l-istore-heat-pump/)), and Reclaim describes its CO2 system as working like a **3–4 kWh battery** for your home ([Reclaim brochure](https://reclaimenergy.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Residential-Consumer-Brochure.pdf)).

There's a bonus, too. Rheem's AmbiHeat manual notes the heat pump is more efficient during the day when the air is warmer, so a midday window helps twice: free solar and better efficiency.

## Self-consumption vs feed-in tariffs: the 2026 numbers

When your panels make more than you're using, the surplus goes to the grid and you get the feed-in tariff. When you need power at night, you buy it back at your usage rate. The gap between those two prices is what a solar-timed heat pump captures. Here's what the official 2026–27 figures say:

| Where | Official 2026–27 figure | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regional Queensland (Ergon area) | Feed-in tariff **6.006 c/kWh**, 1 July 2026 to 30 June 2027 (down from 8.660 c/kWh) | [QCA gazette, 5 June 2026](https://www.qca.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/050626-29-extra-gazette.pdf); [QCA fact sheet](https://www.qca.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/solar-fit-fact-sheet-june-2026.pdf) |
| New South Wales | IPART all-day benchmark **3.4 to 6.5 c/kWh** (2025–26 was 4.8 to 7.3). Retailers don't have to offer a rate in this range | [IPART solar feed-in tariffs](https://www.ipart.nsw.gov.au/Home/Industries/Energy/Retail-prices/Solar-Energy) |
| NSW, by time of day | Midday exports are worth the least. Ausgrid's 2026–27 benchmark is **3.9 to 4 c/kWh** from 10 am to 3 pm, against **17.2 to 18.7 c/kWh** from 4 pm to 9 pm | [IPART time-of-day fact sheet](https://www.ipart.nsw.gov.au/documents/fact-sheet/fact-sheet-time-day-solar-feed-tariff-benchmarks-2026-27) |
| Victoria | No minimum feed-in tariff since 1 July 2025. Retailers set their own, but it can't be below zero | [Essential Services Commission](https://www.esc.vic.gov.au/electricity-and-gas/electricity-and-gas-tariffs-and-benchmarks/minimum-feed-tariff) |

Now compare those with what you pay for power. In regional Queensland the QCA's 2026–27 notified prices are **26.268 c/kWh** on the general Tariff 11 and **15.240 c/kWh** on controlled-load Tariff 33, both excluding GST (same gazette). IPART's 2026–27 announcement makes the point directly: most customers get the best value from their solar by using it rather than exporting it.

### A worked example (our calculation)

Take a household heating **150 L a day** by 45 °C, with a heat pump at a cautious seasonal COP of 3.5. That's about **2.2 kWh of electricity a day**, or about **820 kWh a year** (the same assumptions as our [running costs guide](/blog/heat-pump-vs-electric-storage-running-costs)). In regional Queensland:

| How the heat pump is powered | Rate used (incl. GST where it applies) | Cost of that power a year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bought on Tariff 11 (general) | about 28.9c/kWh | about $237 |
| Bought on Tariff 33 (controlled load) | about 16.8c/kWh | about $137 |
| Your own solar (feed-in you give up) | 6.006c/kWh | about $49 |

GST-inclusive rates are our calculation (gazette rate × 1.1). Your numbers depend on your tariff, your usage and how much of the heating really lands in the solar window. On cloudy days the heat pump will pull some power from the grid. Use the rates on your own bill to redo the sums.

If you're on a time-of-use plan, look at the daytime rate as well. In the same gazette, regional Queensland's residential time-of-use Tariff 12E charges **6.367 c/kWh** (excluding GST) from 11 am to 4 pm. So a midday timer can pay off even on days your panels are busy with other loads.

## The Solar Sharer Offer: free power in the middle of the day

Since **1 July 2026**, households with a smart meter in NSW, South East Queensland and South Australia can opt in to the federal **Solar Sharer Offer**. According to [energy.gov.au](https://www.energy.gov.au/news/new-energy-plan-daily-window-free-electricity) and the [Australian Energy Regulator](https://www.aer.gov.au/industry/retail/default-market-offer), it gives **3 hours of free electricity** every day, from **11 am to 2 pm** in NSW and South East Queensland and **12 pm to 3 pm** in South Australia, with a reasonable-use cap of **24 kWh** a day. It's available to renters and homeowners, with or without solar.

A heat pump is one of the easiest appliances to shift into that window. Two catches: it's an opt-in plan, and the AER's [2026–27 determination](https://www.aer.gov.au/system/files/2026-05/AER%20-%20Final%20determination%20-%20Default%20market%20offer%202026%E2%80%9327.pdf) says usage outside the free period is **1 to 4 c/kWh higher** than the matching standard time-of-use offer. Compare it against your current plan on [Energy Made Easy](https://www.energymadeeasy.gov.au/) before switching.

## Setting a solar-soak timer

Most heat pumps let you set one or two heating windows a day. A good starting point:

1. **Find your solar peak.** Open your inverter app on a sunny day and see when output is highest and the house is using least. For most homes that's somewhere around late morning to mid-afternoon.
2. **Make the window long enough.** Rheem's AmbiHeat installation manual (download it from the [AmbiHeat page](https://www.rheem.com.au/rheem/products/Residential/Heat-Pumps/Ambiheat-/Rheem-Ambiheat-HDc270-Heat-Pump-(with-R513a-refrigerant)/p/571D270R5)) says the timer window must be long enough to heat the whole tank from cold to 60 °C, and that can take **up to six hours** in a cold climate in winter. Reclaim's install manual says a full tank takes about three hours. Start generous and trim it back.
3. **Avoid the evening peak.** If you're on a time-of-use plan, don't let the heat pump run in the peak (for example 4 pm to 9 pm on regional Queensland's Tariff 12E).
4. **Keep it hot enough.** Water must still be stored at 60 °C or more to control legionella. Rheem's manual says a timer set-up meets this as long as the heat pump runs long enough each day.
5. **Watch the boost button.** Boost and backup elements draw far more than the heat pump itself (1.6 to 2.4 kW on some models), so use them sparingly outside the solar window.
6. **Re-check in winter.** Shorter days, cloud and colder air mean slower heating. Sanden notes its heat pump unit will run at night in cold-climate regions when the air drops below 3 °C, and its brochure recommends continuous electricity, not off-peak, in cold climates.

Your installer should set the clock and timer on the day. Ask them to show you how to change it.

Less than people expect. Here are the rated electrical inputs from the manufacturer documents (test conditions differ, so treat them as a rough guide):

| Reclaim Energy CO2 315L | Not listed in the brochure; average use about 3 kWh a day | No element needed | [Reclaim brochure](https://reclaimenergy.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Residential-Consumer-Brochure.pdf) |
| Sanden Eco Plus 315L | 0.84 kW (at 32.45 °C ambient) | No element needed | [Sanden brochure](https://www.sanden-hot-water.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/SANDEN_Brochure_25.pdf) |

So the question isn't really "is my system big enough?", it's "is there about 1 kW to spare in the middle of the day?" If your inverter app shows you exporting at least that much around midday on most days, the heat pump will run mostly on solar during its window. If the fridge, pool pump, air conditioner and EV charger already soak up your midday output, you'll still save, just less.

If you're adding solar or a battery at the same time, mention the heat pump to your solar installer so they size for it. And if your existing solar is small or shaded, a heat pump on an off-peak or time-of-use tariff is still a big step down from an electric element.

## Rebates still apply when you have solar

Having solar panels doesn't stop you claiming hot water rebates. Official figures checked on 25 October 2026:

- **Federal STCs:** any air source heat pump on the [CER register](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-renewable-energy-systems/solar-water-heaters-and-air-source-heat-pumps) with a tank of **less than 425 L** can earn STCs, and you must create them within 12 months of installation if you claim them yourself. The STC Clearing House price is capped at **$40 excluding GST** ([CER](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-technology-certificates/buy-and-sell-small-scale-technology-certificates), last updated 14 September 2026). Most installers take them off the price. See [how STCs work](/blog/how-stcs-work-when-you-replace-your-hot-water).
- **New South Wales:** the [Hot Water Upgrade Incentive](https://www.energy.nsw.gov.au/households/grants-rebates/household-energy-saving-upgrades/hot-water-upgrade-incentive) (updated 6 February 2026) lists an indicative discount of up to **$640** when replacing an electric water heater, or up to **$330** when replacing gas, with a minimum **5-year warranty** covering the heat pump unit, tank and components. More in our [NSW rebate guide](/blog/heat-pump-hot-water-rebate-nsw-2026).
- **Victoria:** the [Solar Victoria hot water rebate](https://www.solar.vic.gov.au/hot-water-rebate) (last updated 21 October 2026) is up to **$1,000**, or up to **$1,400** for locally made products. The locally made list includes the **Reclaim REHP-CO2-315GL-V2**, **Sanden GAUS-315FQTS** and **Rheem 571D270**. The [Victorian Energy Upgrades](https://www.energy.vic.gov.au/households/victorian-energy-upgrades-for-households/hot-water-systems) program (page updated 15 October 2026) lists an indicative **$630** discount when replacing an inefficient electric system with a heat pump, or **$560** when replacing gas.

## Heat pump or solar hot water?

If you already have solar panels, a heat pump on a midday timer usually makes more sense than adding solar hot water collectors to the roof. It needs no roof space, works at night and on cloudy days, and uses the panels you've already paid for. Our [heat pump vs solar hot water guide](/blog/heat-pump-vs-solar-hot-water) covers the trade-offs.

## Questions to ask your installer

- Can this model run on a **timer or app**, and will you set the solar window for me on the day?
- How long does it take to heat a **full tank from cold** in winter here, and is my window long enough?
- Does it have a **backup or boost element**, and how much power does it draw?
- If I want it to respond to my solar automatically, what **wiring, relay or controller** does that need?
- Will connecting a **solar diverter** or energy manager affect the warranty?
- Should I stay on a **controlled-load tariff**, move to a time-of-use plan, or look at the **Solar Sharer Offer**?
- How many **STCs** does it earn in my postcode, and what price are you using for them?
