Short answer: **in our Queensland example, a heat pump replacing electric hot water pays for its extra cost in roughly one to five years, and it pays back the whole after-STC price in roughly 4.5 to 12 years on a general tariff (longer on a cheap controlled-load tariff), depending on the model.** Which of those two numbers matters to you depends on whether your old tank still works. This guide shows you how to work out your own figure in four steps, using official rebate pages checked on **4 November 2026**.

## The payback formula

**Payback (years) = (price of the heat pump after rebates − price of the alternative) ÷ yearly saving**

You need three numbers: what the heat pump costs you after rebates, what you'd otherwise have paid, and how many dollars a year it saves. The next four steps get each of them.

## Step 1: Work out how much hot water you use

Heating water takes about **2.6 kWh of heat for every 50 L** raised by 45 °C, for example from 15 °C to 60 °C. Our [electric running costs guide](/blog/heat-pump-vs-electric-storage-running-costs) uses a daily figure per household. As a rough starting point, use the table below. Everyone's use differs, and our [sizing guide](/blog/what-size-heat-pump-do-i-need) helps you pick a tank.

| Hot water heated per day | Electric tank | Heat pump (seasonal COP 3.5) | Electricity saved a year |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 100 L | 5.2 kWh | 1.5 kWh | about 1,360 kWh |
| 150 L | 7.9 kWh | 2.2 kWh | about 2,050 kWh |
| 200 L | 10.5 kWh | 3.0 kWh | about 2,730 kWh |

An electric element turns each kWh into one kWh of heat. A heat pump divides that by its COP. We use a cautious seasonal COP of 3.5, and we leave out standing heat losses from the tank, which flatters the electric tank.

## Step 2: Turn the electricity saved into dollars

Multiply the kWh saved by your hot water rate from your bill. Our example uses the Queensland Competition Authority's 2026-27 notified prices for regional Queensland, which exclude GST. The GST-inclusive rates are our calculation (× 1.1):

| Tariff | Price (ex GST) | Price (inc GST) | Saving at 2,050 kWh a year (150 L a day) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Tariff 11, general | 26.268 c/kWh | about 28.9 c/kWh | about **$590 a year** |
| Tariff 33, controlled load | 15.240 c/kWh | about 16.8 c/kWh | about **$340 a year** |

Outside regional Queensland, use the rate on your own bill. If you're a Victorian replacing gas, Solar Victoria says switching from gas to an efficient electric hot water system can save households **around $400 a year** ([Solar Victoria](https://www.solar.vic.gov.au/hot-water-rebate), updated 20 October 2026). That's a general figure, not a quote for your home.

## Step 3: Work out what you pay after rebates

Start with the installed price on your written quote, then take off:

- **Federal STCs**, Australia-wide. A heat pump under 425 L can create STCs, and the Clean Energy Regulator says STCs have a clearing house price of **$40 ex GST** each ([CER, updated 14 September 2026](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-technology-certificates/buy-and-sell-small-scale-technology-certificates)). The number you get steps down each year, so a later install gets fewer. See [how STCs work when you replace your hot water](/blog/how-stcs-work-when-you-replace-your-hot-water).
- **NSW:** the [Hot Water Upgrade Incentive](https://www.energy.nsw.gov.au/households/grants-rebates/household-energy-saving-upgrades/hot-water-upgrade-incentive) lists up to **$640** when you replace an electric water heater with a heat pump, and up to **$330** when you replace a gas one (page updated 6 February 2026).
- **Victoria:** [Solar Victoria's hot water rebate](https://www.solar.vic.gov.au/hot-water-rebate) is up to **$1,000**, or up to **$1,400** for locally made products, for eligible owner-occupiers. Victorian Energy Upgrades discounts can stack on top.

## Step 4: Divide

### Version A: against keeping a working electric tank

Here the "alternative" costs you nothing today, so payback is the whole after-STC price divided by the yearly saving. This is the toughest test, and it's the one to use if you're deciding whether to replace a tank that still works.

| Model | Price after STCs | Payback on Tariff 11 ($590 a year) | Payback on Tariff 33 ($340 a year) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Emerald 220L | $2,650 | about 4.5 years | about 7.8 years |
| iStore 270L | $2,990 | about 5.1 years | about 8.8 years |
| Rheem AmbiHeat 270L | $3,690 | about 6.3 years | about 10.9 years |
| Stiebel WWK 302 H | $5,130 | about 8.7 years | about 15.1 years |
| Reclaim CO2 315L | $5,890 | about 10 years | about 17.3 years |
| Sanden Eco Plus 315L | $6,850 | about 11.6 years | about 20.1 years |

These figures use a 150 L a day household, so a bigger tank for a bigger family isn't directly comparable. They also assume prices stay flat, and they don't count the tank's own running or replacement costs. Treat them as a way to compare options, not a promise.

### Version B: against buying a new electric tank

If your tank has failed, or is about to, the honest comparison is with the new tank you'd otherwise buy. Ask a plumber for a like-for-like quote for a new electric tank, then use this:

**Payback = (heat pump price after STCs − new electric tank price) ÷ yearly saving**

For illustration only, assume a new electric tank would cost **$2,000** installed. That's our assumption, not a sourced price, so replace it with your own quote.

| Model | Extra cost vs a $2,000 tank | Payback on Tariff 11 | Payback on Tariff 33 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Emerald 220L | $650 | about 1.1 years | about 1.9 years |
| iStore 270L | $990 | about 1.7 years | about 2.9 years |
| Rheem AmbiHeat 270L | $1,690 | about 2.9 years | about 5.0 years |

## What moves your payback the most

- **Your tariff.** In our example, Tariff 11 pays back about 1.7 times faster than Tariff 33. A heat pump saves the same kWh either way, but each kWh is worth more on a general tariff. Our [Queensland guide](/blog/is-a-heat-pump-worth-it-in-queensland) covers that further.
- **Household size.** Savings scale with how much hot water you use. A smaller household uses less hot water, so it saves fewer kWh and takes longer to pay back.
- **Rebates.** Every dollar off the price shortens payback. NSW's up to $640 for replacing electric, or Victoria's $1,000 to $1,400, can take about a year or more off the Version A figures. For example, on our guide prices a Victorian replacing gas with a Rheem AmbiHeat, with STCs of $640 and a VEU discount of $560, then the $1,400 locally made rebate, would pay about $1,690. At Solar Victoria's general figure of $400 a year, that's about 4.2 years. Check eligibility on your own postcode in our [rebate checker](/rebates) and see [rebates by state](/blog/heat-pump-hot-water-rebates-by-state).
- **Rooftop solar.** If you run the heat pump on a midday timer, the electricity can cost you the feed-in tariff instead of the retail rate. Regional Queensland's regulated feed-in tariff for 2026-27 is 6.006 c/kWh. That makes the running cost very low and shortens payback.
- **Model choice.** A cheaper model pays back sooner, but a pricier one may suit a cold climate or a bigger household better. See our [cold climate guide](/blog/best-heat-pump-cold-climate) and [cost guide](/blog/heat-pump-hot-water-cost-australia).

## Questions to ask your installer

- Can you show the price, STCs and any state discount as separate lines on the quote?
- What would a new electric tank, or a gas replacement, cost installed, so I can compare like with like?
- Does the model suit my tariff, including any controlled-load hours?
- Which timer or solar settings do you recommend?
- Are there any extra costs, such as a new circuit, a switchboard upgrade or a longer pipe run?
