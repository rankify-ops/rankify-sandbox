If you live in Queensland and your hot water is on "Tariff 31" or "Tariff 33", you're on an economy tariff: a cheaper rate for an appliance the network can switch off at busy times. When you swap an old electric tank for a heat pump, which one you're on matters more than most people expect. Here's what each tariff is, what they cost in 2026–27, and why a heat pump usually belongs on Tariff 33. Official pages were checked on **26 October 2026**.

## The short answer

- **Tariff 31** gives power for **at least 8 hours a day**, mostly overnight. It suits a big conventional electric tank that heats fast.
- **Tariff 33** gives power for **at least 16 hours a day**, usually outside the 4 pm to 9 pm peak. It suits a heat pump, which heats more slowly.
- **In regional Queensland they now cost almost the same.** Ergon Energy Retail's 2026–27 rates are **16.663c/kWh** (Tariff 31) and **16.764c/kWh** (Tariff 33), including GST ([Ergon economy tariffs](https://www.ergon.com.au/retail/residential/tariffs-and-prices/economy-tariffs)).
- **So for a heat pump, Tariff 33 is usually the easy call:** twice the heating time for about a tenth of a cent more per kWh.

## What are Tariff 31 and Tariff 33?

Every Queensland home has a **primary tariff** for general power, such as flat-rate Tariff 11 or a time-of-use plan. An **economy tariff** (also called controlled load or a secondary tariff) is an optional extra for appliances that don't need power around the clock, like a hot water system or pool pump. The appliance is wired on its own circuit back to the meter, and the network can switch it off to manage demand. In return, you pay a lower rate.

Your retailer might use a different name on your bill:

| | Tariff 31 | Tariff 33 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Other names | Super Economy, Night Rate, Controlled Load 1 (CL1) | Economy, Controlled Load 2 (CL2) |
| Minimum supply | **8 hours a day** | **16 hours a day** |
| Typical times (Energex) | Around 10 pm to 7 am | Usually outside 4 pm to 9 pm |
| Ergon's suggested hot water sizes | Electric hot water 250 L+ | Electric hot water 125 L+, **heat pumps 270 L+**, solar hot water 160 L+ |
| 2026–27 Ergon rate (incl. GST) | **$0.16663/kWh** | **$0.16764/kWh** |

Sources: [Energex economy tariffs](https://www.energex.com.au/manage-your-energy/tariffs/economy-tariffs), [Ergon economy tariffs](https://www.ergon.com.au/retail/residential/tariffs-and-prices/economy-tariffs) and the QCA's [2026–27 notified prices gazette](https://www.qca.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/050626-29-extra-gazette.pdf) (5 June 2026).

### 16 hours or 18 hours?

You'll see two figures for Tariff 33. The QCA gazette, Ergon and Energex all say **at least 16 hours a day**. The Queensland Government's [electricity bill tips page](https://www.qld.gov.au/housing/buying-owning-home/energy-water-home/electricity/reduce-electricity-bill) says **at least 18 hours**. We've gone with the 16 hours in the network and pricing documents, because that's the minimum the network commits to. In practice many homes get more.

The gazette also notes that Queensland customers connected to the Essential Energy network get shorter minimums: 5 hours on Tariff 31 and 10 hours on Tariff 33.

## When is power on? (Tariff 31 and 33 times in Queensland)

There's no fixed timetable you can look up for your street. Ergon says supply times "vary and can change daily". Energex says Tariff 31 runs around 10 pm to 7 am and Tariff 33 usually outside 4 pm to 9 pm, and both can change with the network's needs.

One change is worth knowing about if you're in South East Queensland. Energex says it has been moving economy tariff timing away from managing the evening peak and towards **solar soaking** in the middle of the day, so supply is typically interrupted during the morning peak and resumed around midday. It suggests updating any appliance timer to avoid the morning and evening peaks.

That's good news for a heat pump on Tariff 33: midday is when the air is warmest and the heat pump is most efficient.

## Why a heat pump usually belongs on Tariff 33

A conventional electric tank has a big heating element that can reheat a large tank in a few hours overnight. A heat pump works differently. It uses a compressor of around 0.5 to 1 kW to pull heat from the air, so it uses far less power, but it can take longer to heat a full tank, especially on a cold night.

That's why Ergon lists heat pumps of 270 L or more under Tariff 33, not Tariff 31. And it lines up with what manufacturers say:

- **Rheem AmbiHeat:** the installation manual (on the [AmbiHeat page](https://www.rheem.com.au/rheem/products/Residential/Heat-Pumps/Ambiheat-/Rheem-Ambiheat-HDc270-Heat-Pump-(with-R513a-refrigerant)/p/571D270R5)) says the heat pump is more efficient during the day, and a heating window must be long enough to heat the full tank to 60 °C, which can take **up to six hours** in a cold climate in winter. An 8-hour overnight window leaves little room for a heavy-use day.
- **Sanden Eco Plus:** its [brochure](https://www.sanden-hot-water.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/SANDEN_Brochure_25.pdf) recommends continuous electricity, not off-peak, in cold climates, and notes the unit will run at night when the air drops below 3 °C.
- **Reclaim Energy CO2:** the [brochure](https://reclaimenergy.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/Residential-Consumer-Brochure.pdf) lists presets for off-peak, continuous and solar operation, and its install manual says a full tank takes about three hours.

Every brand sets its own rules, and they change between models. Your installer should check the installation manual for the exact model before wiring it to an economy tariff.

## What it costs: a worked example (our calculation)

Same assumptions as our [running costs guide](/blog/heat-pump-vs-electric-storage-running-costs): a household heating **150 L a day** by 45 °C. That's about 7.85 kWh of heat a day. An electric element turns that into about **2,865 kWh a year**. A heat pump at a cautious seasonal COP of 3.5 needs about **820 kWh a year**. Using Ergon Energy Retail's 2026–27 rates (including GST):

| System and tariff | Rate | Cost a year |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Old electric tank on Tariff 31 | $0.16663/kWh | about **$477** |
| Heat pump on Tariff 11 (general) | $0.28895/kWh | about **$237** |
| Heat pump on Tariff 31 | $0.16663/kWh | about **$137** |
| Heat pump on Tariff 33 | $0.16764/kWh | about **$137** |
| Heat pump in the Tariff 12E (Solar Soaker) day window, 11 am to 4 pm | $0.07004/kWh | about **$57** |

A few things jump out:

- **Swapping the tank is the big win.** Moving from an electric tank on Tariff 31 to a heat pump on Tariff 33 saves about **$340 a year** in this example.
- **Tariff 31 vs 33 is now a rounding error on price** in regional Queensland, about 80 cents a year here. The real difference is heating time.
- **Leaving a heat pump on general power costs about $100 a year more** than putting it on Tariff 33, though it gives you full control of the timer. Weigh that against the one-off cost of adding an economy tariff (below).
- **Time-of-use plans can be cheaper again,** but Tariff 12E is a whole-of-home tariff with a **$0.47207/kWh** peak from 4 pm to 9 pm, so it only pays if you can keep the rest of the house out of the evening peak. Since 1 July 2026 there's also Ergon's Solar Sharer tariff (12F) with free power from 11 am to 2 pm, up to 24 kWh a day.

These are our own sums from the published rates, not a quote. Your usage, tank size and climate will change them, so redo the sums with the rates on your own bill. In South East Queensland, retailers set their own prices for Tariffs 31 and 33, so compare plans on [Energy Made Easy](https://www.energymadeeasy.gov.au/).

## Switching tariffs: what it involves

- **You need a licensed electrician.** Both Ergon and Energex say a hot water system on an economy tariff must be hard-wired on a dedicated circuit back to the meter. If your old tank was already on Tariff 31 or 33, the heat pump can usually go on the same circuit, as long as the model allows it.
- **There may be a meter fee.** Ergon charges a one-off **$208.71 including GST** for standard residential properties to add an economy tariff, billed to your electricity account, on top of the electrician's time. In South East Queensland, ask your retailer what it charges.
- **Moving from 31 to 33** is usually a paperwork change for your electrician and retailer, but ask before assuming there's no cost.
- **Smart meters:** if you're also changing your primary tariff to time-of-use, you'll need a smart meter. Ergon says there's no extra charge for one, but your switchboard must be compliant.

Ask your installer to sort the electrical side as part of the job. Most heat pump installs in Queensland already include a licensed electrician to disconnect the old tank and connect the new one.

## Rebates in Queensland

There's no general Queensland household rebate for hot water in 2026. The Climate Smart Energy Savers rebate ended on 30 June 2025. The federal STC discount still applies: any air source heat pump on the [Clean Energy Regulator register](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-renewable-energy-systems/solar-water-heaters-and-air-source-heat-pumps) with a tank under **425 L** can earn STCs, and the Clearing House price is capped at **$40 excluding GST** ([CER](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-technology-certificates/buy-and-sell-small-scale-technology-certificates)). Most installers take them off the price. The full picture is in our [Queensland heat pump guide](/blog/is-a-heat-pump-worth-it-in-queensland), [how STCs work](/blog/how-stcs-work-when-you-replace-your-hot-water) and the [rebate checker](/rebates).

## Got solar? Think about midday

If you have rooftop solar, a heat pump on a midday timer can run largely on your own power, which is worth more than the 2026–27 regional feed-in tariff of 6.006c/kWh. You can do that on general power with a timer, or on Tariff 33 now that Energex's economy windows lean towards the middle of the day. Our [solar panels guide](/blog/heat-pump-vs-solar-hot-water) covers timer settings.

## Questions to ask your installer

- Is my current hot water on **Tariff 31, Tariff 33 or general power**? (It's on your bill.)
- What does the **installation manual** for this model say about controlled-load supply?
- Can it heat a **full tank in the window** my tariff gives, on a cold morning?
- Will the electrician need to **change the circuit or meter**, and what will that cost?
- Should I set a **timer**, and will you set it on the day?
- How many **STCs** does it earn at my postcode, and what price are you using?
