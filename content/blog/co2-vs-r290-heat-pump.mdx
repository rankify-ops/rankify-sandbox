Look closely at a heat pump hot water quote and you'll see a refrigerant named: **CO2** (also written R744), **R290** (propane), or an older synthetic gas like **R134a** or **R513a**. The refrigerant is the fluid that soaks up heat from the air and hands it to your water, and it shapes the price, the noise, the cold-weather rating and the climate impact. Here's how CO2 and R290 compare, using official and manufacturer pages checked on **24 October 2026**.

## The short answer

- **Pick R290** if you want the best value and live somewhere mild. Our two R290 models start at a **$3,290** guide price and are rated to **−5 °C to −7 °C**.
- **Pick CO2** if you get regular frosts, the unit has to sit near a bedroom, or you want the tank inside. Our two CO2 models are rated to **−10 °C** at **37 dB**, but start at **$6,490**.
- **Both are low-impact natural refrigerants.** The bigger climate step is choosing either one over an older HFC gas.
- **Rebates don't pick the winner.** STCs and state discounts depend on the model and your postcode, with one Victorian exception covered below.

## What the refrigerant actually does

A heat pump works like a fridge in reverse. A refrigerant is pumped around a sealed loop, picking up heat from the outside air at the evaporator, getting compressed to push its temperature up, and then releasing that heat into your water. The refrigerant choice affects how hot it can push the water, how well it copes with cold air, and what happens to the climate if the gas ever leaks.

## CO2 and R290 at a glance

The federal Department of Climate Change, Energy, the Environment and Water (DCCEEW) publishes [global warming potential values and safety classifications](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/environment/protection/ozone/rac/global-warming-potential-values-hfc-refrigerants) for refrigerants:

| Refrigerant | What it is | Global warming potential | Safety class |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| R744 (CO2) | Carbon dioxide, a natural refrigerant | Under 5 | A1 (non-flammable, lower toxicity) |
| R290 | Propane, a natural refrigerant | Under 5 | A3 (highly flammable) |
| R134a | Synthetic HFC | 1,430 | A1 |
| R513A | HFC/HFO blend | 631 ([DCCEEW blends table](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/sites/default/files/env/pages/78ecf1c3-b519-4bbb-b415-a45b89e51a9e/files/global-warming-potential-table-hfcs-blends.pdf)) | A1 |

Global warming potential (GWP) compares how much a gas warms the planet against the same weight of carbon dioxide over 100 years. So a kilogram of R134a leaking is roughly 1,430 times worse than a kilogram of CO2, while R290 is in the same very low band as CO2.

## Not sure where HFCs fit? A quick word on the phase-down

Australia is cutting imports of bulk HFC gases through a quota system that started on 1 January 2018. DCCEEW's [HFC phase-down FAQ](https://www.dcceew.gov.au/environment/protection/ozone/hfc-phase-down/hfc-phase-down-faqs) says existing equipment isn't affected, gas for servicing it will stay available, and consumers won't be forced to replace what they've got. It also names carbon dioxide and hydrocarbons (like propane) as low-GWP alternatives already on the market.

In plain terms: an R134a or R513a heat pump is still legal and still earns STCs. But if you're buying new and the climate impact matters to you, CO2 and R290 are the cleaner choices.

## CO2 vs R290, point by point

### Climate impact

A tie, more or less. DCCEEW puts both CO2 and propane in the group of refrigerants with a GWP under 5. Either one is a big step down from R134a (1,430) or R513A (631). The day-to-day climate impact of any heat pump comes mostly from the electricity it uses, so running it on solar or an off-peak tariff matters more than which natural refrigerant it carries.

### Cold weather

In our range, CO2 wins. Both CO2 models are rated down to **−10 °C**. The R290 models are rated to **−7 °C** (Emerald) and **−5 °C** (iStore). In most of Queensland, coastal New South Wales, Perth and Adelaide, the difference rarely comes into play. In Canberra, Tasmania, the Victorian high country or the New England tablelands, it's worth paying attention to. See our [best heat pumps for cold climates](/blog/best-heat-pump-cold-climate) and [do heat pumps work in winter?](/blog/do-heat-pumps-work-in-winter-australia).

### Noise

CO2 wins in our range, but that's partly down to the layout. Both CO2 models are split systems rated at **37 dB**, with the compressor and fan in a separate outdoor unit. The R290 models are all-in-one units at **47 to 49 dB(A)**. Brands don't all measure noise the same way, so treat small gaps with care, but 10 dB or more is a noticeable difference. More tips in our [noise and placement guide](/blog/heat-pump-hot-water-noise-and-placement).

### Safety

This is where the two really differ. DCCEEW classes R290 as **A3, highly flammable**, and CO2 as **A1**. In a hot water heat pump, the propane charge is small and sealed inside the unit at the factory, which is why R290 units are designed for a well-ventilated outdoor spot. iStore, for example, says its 270L is [designed for a well-ventilated outdoor location](https://heatpumps.istore.net.au/270l-istore-heat-pump/). Stick to the manufacturer's clearances, and don't let anyone install an R290 unit in a cupboard or enclosed space unless the manual allows it.

CO2 runs at much higher pressure, which is handled inside the sealed outdoor unit. On the Reclaim and Sanden systems, the CO2 circuit never leaves that unit: hot water is pumped through water pipes to the tank, and Sanden's [brochure](https://www.sanden-hot-water.com.au/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/SANDEN_Brochure_25.pdf) says installation is by a **plumber and electrician only**.

### Installation and who can service it

Both types are usually installed by a licensed plumber and electrician. The AIRAH [flammable refrigerants safety guide](https://airah.org.au/Common/Uploaded%20files/Resources/FRSG/AIRAH-Flammable-Refrigerants-Safety-Guide-2026-digital.pdf) notes that natural refrigerants (hydrocarbons, ammonia and CO2) aren't covered by the ARCtick refrigerant handling licence that applies to HFCs, and DCCEEW says technicians working with alternative gases should be appropriately trained. So if a sealed refrigerant circuit ever needs repair, it's a job for the manufacturer's service agent, not a general handyman.

The bigger install difference in our range is the layout: R290 models are one integrated unit, while our CO2 models are splits with two parts and pipes between them. Our [installation day guide](/blog/how-long-does-heat-pump-installation-take) covers what usually happens.

### Efficiency

Hard to call from the brochures. Brands quote efficiency (COP) at different test conditions, so the numbers don't line up. Reclaim quotes COP 6.02 at 32 °C, while iStore quotes 4.8 at 20 °C, and a warmer test day always gives a higher number. Both refrigerants are efficient enough to cut water heating energy by a large margin compared with an electric element. For running cost estimates, see our [heat pump vs electric storage running costs](/blog/heat-pump-vs-electric-storage-running-costs) guide.

### Price

R290 wins clearly. On our guide prices the R290 models are **$3,290 to $3,590** and the CO2 models **$6,490 to $7,490**, before rebates. STCs are similar (15 to 16 in zone 3 for both groups), so they don't close the gap, though Victoria's locally made bonus narrows it a little. For wider price ranges, see our [heat pump cost guide](/blog/heat-pump-hot-water-cost-australia).

### Warranty

Warranty depends on the brand and version, not the refrigerant. When we checked on 23 October 2026, Reclaim listed **10 years parts and labour** on its Wi-Fi heat pump unit ([Reclaim warranties](https://reclaimenergy.com.au/warranties/)), and Sanden listed **6 years** on the standard heat pump unit and **15 years** on its stainless tanks (10 years full replacement, then 5 years pro rata) ([Sanden warranty](https://www.sanden-hot-water.com.au/warranty-information/)). iStore's 270L carries **5 years** parts and labour. Check the warranty on the exact model number on your quote.

## Rebates for CO2 and R290 heat pumps

**Federal STCs:** any air source heat pump on the [Clean Energy Regulator's register](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-renewable-energy-systems/solar-water-heaters-and-air-source-heat-pumps) with a tank of **less than 425 L** can earn STCs, whatever its refrigerant. The count depends on the model and your zone. The STC Clearing House price is capped at **$40 excluding GST** ([CER](https://cer.gov.au/schemes/renewable-energy-target/small-scale-renewable-energy-scheme/small-scale-technology-certificates/buy-and-sell-small-scale-technology-certificates), last updated 14 September 2026). Installers usually sell for a bit less on the open market. More in [how STCs work](/blog/how-stcs-work-when-you-replace-your-hot-water).

**New South Wales:** the [Hot Water Upgrade Incentive](https://www.energy.nsw.gov.au/households/grants-rebates/household-energy-saving-upgrades/hot-water-upgrade-incentive) (page updated 6 February 2026) gives an indicative discount of up to **$640** when replacing an electric water heater, or up to **$330** when replacing gas. The heat pump needs a minimum **5-year warranty** covering the heat pump unit, tank and other components.

**Victoria:** the [Solar Victoria hot water rebate](https://www.solar.vic.gov.au/hot-water-rebate) (page updated 21 October 2026) is 50% of the purchase price, up to **$1,000**, or up to **$1,400** for products on the locally made list. On 24 October 2026 that list included the CO2 **Reclaim REHP-CO2-315GL-V2** and **Sanden GAUS-315FQTS**, and the **Rheem 571D270**, but not the R290 iStore or Emerald models. So in Victoria, an eligible household choosing one of our CO2 models could get up to $400 more from Solar Victoria. The [Victorian Energy Upgrades](https://www.energy.vic.gov.au/households/victorian-energy-upgrades-for-households/hot-water-systems) program also offers a discount through accredited providers; check that page for the current amount.

Other states and territories are covered in our [rebates by state guide](/blog/heat-pump-hot-water-rebates-by-state), or check your postcode in the [rebate checker](/rebates).

## Which one suits your home?

**An R290 heat pump is usually the better fit if:**

- you're in a mild or warm climate with few frosts
- your tank spot is outdoors with good airflow and isn't right next to a bedroom
- you want the lowest upfront price with a natural refrigerant

**A CO2 heat pump is usually the better fit if:**

- you get regular frosts or cold mornings below −5 °C
- the only spot is near bedrooms or a neighbour's window, so quieter running matters
- you want the tank indoors or in a tight space (our CO2 models are splits)
- you're in Victoria and eligible for the higher locally made rebate

## Questions to ask your installer

- Which **refrigerant** does this exact model use, and is it the current version?
- If it's **R290**, does the planned spot meet the manufacturer's ventilation and clearance rules?
- What's the **cold-weather rating**, and how often does it get that cold here?
- What's the **noise rating** at 1 m, and can it run on a **timer**?
- Who services the sealed refrigerant circuit if something goes wrong?
- How many **STCs** does it earn in my postcode, and what price are you using for them?
- What's the **warranty** on the heat pump unit and the tank, for this exact model number?
