## The short answer

- **There's no official lifespan figure for heat pumps specifically.** The Victorian Government's [hot water buyers guide](https://www.energy.vic.gov.au/victorian-energy-upgrades/products/hot-water-system-discounts/hot-water-buyers-guide) plans around a **10 to 15 year** lifespan for a hot water system, and energy.gov.au says gas water heaters usually last **12 to 15 years** ([energy.gov.au](https://www.energy.gov.au/households/electrification)). We'd treat 10 to 15 years as a planning window, not a promise.
- **The tank usually decides it.** A heat pump is a storage tank plus a small refrigeration unit. Tanks corrode; the compressor, fan and controls can often be repaired.
- **Maintenance is light but it matters.** Lift the relief valve lever about every six months, keep the airflow clear, check the condensate drain, and book a professional service (usually every five years, sooner for some brands).
- **Skipping the service can cost you the warranty.** Several manufacturers say so in writing.

## What actually wears out

A heat pump hot water system has two main parts that age differently:

- **The tank (cylinder).** Most tanks are steel with a glass-enamel lining. The Australian Government's YourHome guide says glass-lined tanks have a **sacrificial anode** that "needs to be replaced every few years", while stainless steel tanks don't need one. It also says tank warranties are typically **5 to 10 years for glass-lined** and **10 to 15 years for stainless** ([YourHome](https://www.yourhome.gov.au/energy/hot-water-systems)). Once a tank leaks, it generally can't be patched.
- **The heat pump unit.** The compressor, fan, evaporator coil, sensors and controller. These are the parts that make it different from an electric tank. Faults here are usually repairable, but parts and labour can be expensive once you're out of warranty.

Then there are the **valves**: the temperature pressure relief (TPR or PTR) valve, and an expansion control valve in some areas. They're cheap, they wear, and most manufacturers want the TPR valve replaced about every five years.

What shortens the life of a heat pump:

- **Water quality.** Hard water, bore water, rainwater or a supply that switches between sources can eat anodes faster or void the tank warranty. Rheem's [AmbiHeat manual](https://www.rheem.com.au/rheem/products/Residential/Heat-Pumps/Ambiheat-/Rheem-Ambiheat-HDc270-Heat-Pump-(with-R513a-refrigerant)/p/571D270R5) only covers its glass-lined cylinder below 2,500 mg/L total dissolved solids (TDS), with the right anode fitted for your water. iStore and Reclaim ask for more frequent anode checks above 600 mg/L.
- **Blocked airflow.** Plants, fences, boxes and leaves around the unit make it work harder. The NSW Government's [heat pump page](https://www.energy.nsw.gov.au/households/upgrades/heat-pump) says to clear debris and vegetation around the outdoor unit to keep air flowing.
- **A neglected relief valve.** Deposits build up under the valve seat if it's never operated.
- **Coastal air.** Salt spray is hard on fins and casings, so placement matters. Our [noise and placement guide](/blog/heat-pump-hot-water-noise-and-placement) covers where to put it.

## The maintenance you can do yourself

These are the owner checks that come up in the manuals we read. Always follow the manual for your exact model.

1. **Lift the relief valve lever every six months.** Rheem, iStore, Reclaim and Stiebel Eltron all say this. Lift it gently, let water run through the drain line for a few seconds, then lower it gently and check it closes. **Stand clear: the water is hot.** If no water flows, or the valve keeps running afterwards, call a plumber. Rheem's manual is blunt that failing to do this may result in the cylinder failing or, under certain circumstances, exploding.
2. **Keep the air intake and outlet clear.** Trim plants back and clear leaves, cobwebs and stored items. Stiebel Eltron asks you to clean the air intake and discharge grilles every six months.
3. **Check the condensate drain.** Heat pumps make water as they pull heat from the air. iStore says up to 5 litres a day is normal above 80% humidity. If the drain is blocked, water pools around the unit.
4. **Watch for warning signs:** pooling water, a valve that drips constantly, rusty or milky water, new grinding or rattling noises, fault codes, or running out of hot water sooner than usual.

The Queensland Building and Construction Commission says home owners and tenants can operate the relief valve themselves, but should take care to avoid scalding and make sure it closes properly ([QBCC home owner's guide to plumbing matters](https://www.qbcc.qld.gov.au/sites/default/files/documents/guide-plumbing-matters-may-2025.pdf), May 2025). Don't open covers, touch wiring or attempt refrigerant work yourself.

## The professional service

The QBCC guide says it's "generally recommended for hot water systems to be serviced every five years by an appropriately licensed person", more often on bore water or rainwater, and that owners should follow the manufacturer's recommendations. A typical service, going by the manuals we checked, covers:

- replacing the **TPR valve** and checking the expansion control valve, if fitted
- inspecting the **anode**, and replacing it if it's worn (glass-lined tanks)
- flushing the tank of sediment
- cleaning dust and residue from the **evaporator**, fan and louvres
- checking the condensate drain, connections and controls

Service work and routine parts like anodes and valves are usually **at your cost**, not covered by the warranty. Rheem and Emerald both say so in their documents. Ask your installer what a service costs in your area when you get your quote.

A few things stand out:

- **Stainless tanks need the least tank maintenance.** No sacrificial anode to swap, and the longest tank warranties (15 years on Sanden and Reclaim stainless).
- **The cheaper glass-lined units need anode attention sooner.** iStore and Emerald both work on a two-year cycle. That's not a reason to avoid them, but budget for it.
- **The Stiebel needs steady power.** Its powered anode protects the tank only while it has power. If your hot water is on a controlled-load tariff that switches it off for long stretches, ask your installer how that affects the anode and its backup batteries.
- **Warranty labour is where they differ most.** Some cover parts and labour for the full term, others cover only the replacement part after the first few years.

## Getting the most years out of a heat pump

- **Pick the right tank for your water.** On bore water, rainwater or in hard water areas, ask the installer about the right anode, a stainless tank or water treatment before you buy. YourHome notes that stainless tanks aren't suitable in some hard water areas, so ask the manufacturer what suits your area.
- **Get it installed properly.** A licensed plumber and electrician (and the right relief valve drain) matter. Building Commission NSW's [advisory note on heat pump installations](https://www.nsw.gov.au/housing-and-construction/compliance-and-regulation/plumbers-and-drainers/standards-and-notes/heat-pump-installations-for-plumbing) sets out how the relief valve drain must be run: a continuous fall, no tap or valve in the line, and a discharge point that won't scald anyone.
- **Register the warranty straight away.** iStore needs it within 6 weeks; Emerald gives you an extra tank year if you register within 3 months.
- **Put the service in the calendar.** Note the install date and when the first anode check or major service is due for your model.
- **Keep the paperwork.** Invoice, model and serial numbers, and any rebate or certificate paperwork. Rheem asks for proof of the rebate or certificates when you claim its 5-year whole-of-product cover.

## Repair or replace?

There's no age cut-off, but here's a sensible way to think about it:

- **Repair** if a valve, sensor, fan or controller has failed, the tank is sound, and especially if the part is still under warranty.
- **Replace** if the tank is leaking or rusting, faults keep coming back, parts are hard to get, or the system no longer suits your household.
- **Plan ahead** if your system is past 10 years or past its tank warranty. Replacing on your schedule gives you time to compare quotes and line up rebates, instead of taking whatever's available on the day the tank bursts.

## Questions to ask your installer

- What's the **tank** made of, and which **anode** suits my water?
- What maintenance does this model need, and **how often**?
- What does a **service** cost, and can you do it?
- What does the warranty cover for **parts and labour**, and for how long?
- Is the warranty **conditional on servicing**? What do I need to keep?
- How do I **register** the warranty, and by when?
- Is there anything about my site (coastal air, bore water, shade, tight space) that will shorten its life?
